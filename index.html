Step 1 of 4 · Orient

Know the genre and its readers.

The overview is the opening block of a portfolio project page. It works like an executive summary, not like an essay introduction. The people reading it are deciding whether to hire you, and they usually decide in under a minute.

Why this step

If you are new to UX, start here. A case study overview is the short section at the top of a portfolio project page. It is closer to an executive summary, a movie logline, or a research abstract than to an introduction. It has to make sense on its own.1

This is a personal portfolio site inside the hiring world. Readers split into two groups: recruiters and hiring managers who scan in seconds, and other designers who read carefully to learn about yor craft.3 Eyetracking research from Nielsen Norman Group found that 79% of web users scan and only 16% read word by word.2

The purposes follow from that: get past the screening pile, show your competency, and make your impact clear fast. Treat the overview as a genre with expectations.4 Meet those expectations and your work reads as fluent, break them and it reads as scattered.

What a newcomer needs to know

  • The parts. Project name, a one-line problem statement, your role, the timeline, the outcomes, and one hero visual.
  • The readers. Recruiters scanning for signal, and designers reading for craft. Write for the scanner first.
  • The purpose. Earn an interview. Every element either helps that or gets cut.
  • The setting. A portfolio site, read alongside dozens of others. Knowing the genre is the baseline.
Why this works

Once you see the overview as a genre, you stop asking "how do I describe my project?" and start asking "what does this genre expect, and how do I make my project fit?"

Step 2 of 4 · Structure

Lead with the results.

Strong overviews put the conclusion first: metrics, your role, and the impact. The step-by-step process story comes later, in the body of the case study.

Why this step

If you learned to write essays, you probably know how to set a scene, walk through a journey, and end with a conclusion. This genre flips that; the conclusion comes first.

This structure is called the inverted pyramid, and it comes from journalism and web writing. The most important information sits at the top: what the impact was, what your role was, and how big the project was. Discovery, ideation, and testing come after.2

The Interaction Design Foundation recommends putting a results summary before the full case study begins, for exactly this reason.1

Do this

  • Lead with a number if you have one. "Reduced support tickets by 38%." Concrete beats abstract.
  • Name your role before naming the team. "Lead designer" is a result.
  • Show the stakes. What changed because the work shipped?
  • Keep the process below. It still matters, just not at the top.
Why this works

The genre values efficiency over chronology because readers may leave in seconds. Putting the punchline first is not a style choice. It is a survival strategy.

Step 3 of 4 · Modes

One hook image, scannable body.

The overview works in two modes at once. A single hero visual sets the tone, and a chunked, labeled text body written in short phrases carries the facts.

Why this step

The first thing a reader sees on a strong portfolio page is one deliberate image: a hero shot of the product, a prototype in action, or a piece of branded artwork. Not three thumbnails, and not a slideshow. Pair it with a short headline that names the problem in a phrase.1

Below the hook, the body is rarely full prose. It is a grid of labeled chunks: Role, Timeline, Team, Tools, Outcome. Each chunk is a small answer to a question a recruiter would have asked. The wording is tight. "Lead product designer," not "I served as the lead product designer on this project." Keywords get bolded so the eye finds them.2

The format is a hybrid. It borrows resume habits (chunked credentials) and web article habits (scannable headers, bolded keys) because its readers come from both worlds.3

Do this

  • Choose one image, not five.
  • Write a short headline that names the problem or the outcome.
  • Label your chunks. Role · Timeline · Team · Tools · Outcome. Recognizable at a glance.
  • Keep paragraphs short. Two lines, then leave room for negative space. Anything longer might get skipped.
Why this works

Attention is a scarce resource in the reading economy. A strong image earns the reader's next three seconds. The chunked body makes sure those seconds land on answers instead of noisy paragraphs.

Step 4 of 4 · Write

Write yours, then trim.

Draft your overview against the earlier steps: the genre parts, results first, one hook plus chunks. Then revise by cutting until a 30-second read is possible.

Why this step

The earlier steps gave you everything the draft needs: a reader with thirty seconds, results at the top, one image that carries the weight, and a chunked scannable body.1

Good revision comes from those facts, not from what feels expressive or what counts as good writing in other genres. Every check below maps to an earlier step.2

Run the list in order, and do not skip the checks that feel obvious! Those are usually the ones newcomers get wrong.

The checklist

  • Include the standard parts. Step 1: project name, problem statement, role, timeline, outcomes, hero visual.
  • Lead with the result. Step 2: a metric, a shipped outcome, or a clear piece of information before any process story.
  • One image, chunked body. Step 3: commit to a single hook and labeled blocks written in short phrases.
  • Then cut half the words. Give it to someone for 30 seconds and ask what they remember. Trim until the answer is your result.
Why this works

Good writing in any genre comes from knowing who it is for and what those readers reward. Write toward the reader you actually have, not the reader you wish you had.
